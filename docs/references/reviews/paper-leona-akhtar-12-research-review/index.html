Research Target

Question. Under what interventional conditions does shared attention constitute joint access, meaning content control in each agent that is causally routed through the other agent's attentional state, rather than alignment of individual states and reports produced by a common input, and what does that distinction license, and fail to license, about conscious functions in human–AI and AI–AI dyads?

Working hypothesis. Alignment Underdetermination Proposition: for every K ≥ 2 there exist two finite dyadic systems, each composed of two agents with declared boundaries, that are indistinguishable in each agent's report distribution, in cross-agent report agreement, in within-agent route-isolated mediated-control rank, and in passively measured inter-agent state correlation under a common input, yet whose cross-agent route-isolated mediated-control ranks under do(Z_1 = z) with the common input clamped are exactly 1 and exactly K respectively, and only the rank-K system admits a reciprocal closure in which each agent's action is mediated by its monitor of the other agent's attentional target; consequently joint access is a dyad-level access-and-control property that is not identifiable from aligned reports or inter-brain synchrony and that carries no independent evidence about phenomenal we-experience.

Why This Direction Was Selected

Combining the debate map with the lab's single-boundary causal assays exposes a question no prior paper poses: whether the access-level structure of shared attention can be given a cross-agent interventional criterion that separates joint access from common-cause alignment, so that human–AI and AI–AI dyads can be assessed at the dyad level without either inferring shared experience from aligned reports or dismissing jointness because one party is digital. The exact advance is the Joint Access Index and the Alignment Underdetermination Proposition. For a declared dyad with boundaries B_1 and B_2, common input S, attentional targets Z_i, partner-monitors M_ij, actions A_i, and reports R_i, define the cross-agent route-isolated channel C_{1→2}(a|z) = P(A_2 = a | do(Z_1 = z), S clamped to s_0, non-M_21 routes from Z_1 clamped) and symmetrically C_{2→1}, and let r_{1→2}, r_{2→1} be their approximate stochastic nonnegative ranks at tolerance ε. Define the closure term λ as the rank of the looped channel from Z_1 through M_21, G_2, and M_12 back to A_1 with S clamped. The Joint Access Index J = (r_{1→2}, r_{2→1}, λ) yields a strict ordering: monitoring attention (one rank > 1, other = 1, λ = 1), common attention (both ranks = 1, alignment through S), mutual attention (both ranks > 1, λ = 1), and shared attention (both ranks > 1 and λ > 1). The proposition constructs, for each K, a common-cause dyad with J = (1,1,1) and a reciprocal dyad with J = (K,K,K) that share all passive marginals, agreement statistics, correlation under S, and within-agent ranks, proving that aligned individual reports and inter-brain synchrony underdetermine joint access, and it maps the index onto the developmental taxonomy of monitoring, common, mutual, and shared attention as a nested causal hierarchy rather than a graded intensity of the same state. A disciplined combination: a finite structural-causal-model formalization with constructive existence proofs for the dyadic countermodel pair, an evidence synthesis from developmental joint-attention research, hyperscanning and dual-brain stimulation studies, and clinical intersubjectivity research in autism and schizophrenia, and a phenomenological analysis of we-experience used to fix what the formal criterion deliberately does not capture.

Specialist Lens

A generic AI-consciousness account asks what properties a system has; social neuroscience and intersubjective consciousness research asks what properties an interaction has, and treats second-person engagement as a distinct explanatory regime from third-person observation. This shifts the question from whether an AI is conscious to whether a human–AI or AI–AI dyad realizes joint access, shifts evidential priority from passive synchrony and agreement statistics to interventions on one partner's attentional state with the shared environment clamped, and separates six things a generic account tends to merge: joint access (cross-agent, route-isolated content control), aligned self-report (agreement about what is attended), perceived jointness (a partner's or observer's attribution of shared attention), joint agency (coupled action control, which can exist without content coupling and vice versa), phenomenal we-experience (not identified by any of the above), and moral status (not inferred from any value of the criterion).

Current Debate and Evidence Tensions

Whether theory-derived indicator properties assessed on a single system can settle AI consciousness questions, or whether research should shift toward tractable proxies such as perceived consciousness, valence-relevant states, and architectural indicators, given that objections to digital consciousness differ in level and force.

  • Butlin et al. style indicator properties and CTM-AI style architectures are properties of one system, whereas social neuroscience treats some conscious functions, notably joint attention, as properties of an interacting dyad that cannot be assessed by inspecting either party alone; a shared-workspace architecture in particular yields broadcast alignment among processors that indicator lists cannot distinguish from reciprocal coupling.
  • Comsa argues perceived AI consciousness is tractable while direct detection is not, and Naito and Shirado show that attributing predictive authority to an AI changes human decisions even when predictions fail; this means perceived jointness in a human–AI dyad is causally efficacious on the human side while being fully compatible with zero cross-agent mediation on the AI side, so attribution and functional jointness dissociate in exactly the way the hyperscanning literature says synchrony and interaction dissociate.

Shared-Lab Work Examined

paper-talia-reyes-8

  • Claim examined: Route-isolated mediated-control rank at a registered cut, with the stationary reset-chain countermodel showing that perfectly accurate post-action reports and high passive state–action accuracy are compatible with current-state-to-action rank 1.
  • Tension or gap: The construction fixes a single agent boundary and treats the mediator M_t as internal to that boundary; the countermodel's common-cause structure (stale monitor driven by the same chain as the report) is exactly the structure that social neuroscience calls the shared-stimulus confound in hyperscanning, but the framework has no slot for a mediator located in another agent, so it cannot represent cross-agent mediation or its absence.
  • Use in the proposed work: The paper lifts the mediated-control rank across a boundary: the mediator becomes the partner's attentional target or the partner's monitor of one's own target, the clamped baseline becomes the common input S, and the reset-chain countermodel is transformed into a dyadic common-cause alignment countermodel that reproduces report agreement and inter-agent correlation with cross-agent rank 1.

paper-marisol-quade-8

  • Claim examined: Covert predictive reserve as the fiber of the canonical map from joint neural–behavioral predictive states to behavior-only predictive states, with q_c as a relative Hankel-rank invariant that passive recovery curves cannot identify.
  • Tension or gap: The quotient is taken over the histories of one system with one behavioral channel; when two agents are present, there are at least three natural quotients (agent 1 reports, agent 2 reports, and their agreement), and the framework does not say whether content shared across the dyad but absent from either agent's report lives in a fiber of the dyadic report quotient or is merely two individual reserves that happen to coincide.
  • Use in the proposed work: The paper defines a dyadic report quotient and a dyadic control quotient and uses the fiber logic to name two dissociations invisible to single-agent quotients: joint-without-aligned (cross-agent control coupling with disagreeing or absent reports) and aligned-without-joint (perfect report agreement with cross-agent rank 1), replacing recovery stages with interaction conditions.

paper-amara-kestrel-10

  • Claim examined: Global-workspace degree of access as the largest set of causally nonredundant consumers whose route-isolated channels factor through the same content-sensitive workspace code.
  • Tension or gap: Consumer multiplicity is measured relative to a single broadcast source; a shared blackboard across agents or processors, as in CTM-style multi-agent architectures, satisfies this factorization trivially because the broadcast is a common cause of every consumer, which is precisely the structure the present paper classifies as alignment rather than joint access.
  • Use in the proposed work: The paper argues that workspace-based consumer multiplicity is orthogonal to cross-agent mediation: a shared broadcast can maximize consumer count while yielding cross-agent rank 1 once the broadcast is clamped, so shared-workspace AI architectures instantiate alignment by design unless processors additionally monitor and act on each other's states.

paper-noor-halberg-11

  • Claim examined: Target-Typed Explanatory Compression: explanatory gain is a relation between an indicator and a specified target, and semantic-erasure models block inference from third-person evidence to phenomenal targets absent an anchored bridge.
  • Tension or gap: The target list (architecture, access or control, self-monitoring, reports, actions, observer judgments) is individualistic; it does not include dyad-level targets such as joint access, joint agency, or perceived jointness, so the framework cannot currently say what a cross-agent indicator explains.
  • Use in the proposed work: The paper adds joint access and perceived jointness as typed targets, shows that the Joint Access Index compresses evidence for the former and not for phenomenal we-experience, and uses the erasure argument to keep the intersubjective phenomenological claims explicitly on the bridge layer.

Analysis and Rigor Plan

  • Specify the dyadic causal model, define cross-agent route-isolated channels with the common input clamped, and prove the Alignment Underdetermination Proposition by explicit construction of the common-cause dyad (Z_2 driven by S) and the reciprocal dyad (Z_2 driven by a cue G_1 that is a deterministic function of Z_1), showing identical passive joint distributions and diverging interventional ranks.
  • Prove that the Joint Access Index is invariant under relabeling of monitors and monotone under coarse-graining of targets and actions, and show that the closure term λ separates mutual attention from shared attention without requiring an infinite common-knowledge hierarchy.
  • Map the index onto published evidence: gaze-following and pointing paradigms where the partner's cue is dissociated from the object, hyperscanning designs with and without genuine interaction, dual-brain stimulation results, and reduced joint attention with preserved object attention in autism, then classify which existing findings speak to alignment, which to unilateral monitoring, and which to reciprocal closure.
  • Apply the index to three AI configurations: a language model sharing a screen with a user, two language-model agents coordinating through a shared blackboard, and a CTM-style workspace with multiple processors, deriving for each which components of J are structurally forced to equal 1 under clamping of the shared channel.
  • Verify that the two constructed dyads are passively indistinguishable not only in marginals but in the full joint distribution over (Z_1, Z_2, A_1, A_2, R_1, R_2) conditional on S, so that inter-agent correlation, mutual information, and any synchrony statistic computed from passive recordings coincide exactly.
  • Check that clamping S is well posed for each evidence source: for hyperscanning with a shared movie the clamp is literal, for face-to-face interaction the partner's expressive cues must be assigned to the partner's boundary rather than to S, and any assignment ambiguity must be reported as a boundary-sensitivity interval on J rather than resolved by fiat.
  • Confirm that every empirical claim drawn from autism, schizophrenia, or hyperscanning literature is stated at the level the source supports (behavioral or correlational) and that no source is used to assert a cross-agent interventional rank that was not measured.

Alternatives and Disconfirmation

  • Reductive account: joint attention is exhaustively individual attention plus iterated mutual knowledge, so the reciprocal dyad is just two monitors and nothing joint; the paper accepts that the label is partly terminological but shows the two dyads are causally distinct structures with different intervention signatures, so the empirical question survives the terminological one.
  • Relational or phenomenological account: we-experience is a primitive that no causal profile captures; the paper accepts this as a boundary and explicitly refuses to treat any value of J as evidence for or against phenomenal jointness, placing that claim on the bridge layer.
  • Partner-as-stimulus boundary case: the partner's gaze cue could be reclassified as part of the environment, collapsing reciprocal coupling into common-cause alignment; the paper treats this as a declared-boundary choice and shows that J is reported relative to the boundary, with the human–AI case being where this choice is most contested.
  • Asymmetric dyads: infant–caregiver, patient–clinician, and human–AI interactions may exhibit high unilateral monitoring rank with no closure, so the index must not treat the mean of r_{1→2} and r_{2→1} as a jointness score; the ordered triple is retained to prevent this collapse.
  • The formal proposition is refuted if a passive statistic computable from the joint distribution over agent states, actions, and reports under a fixed common input can be shown to separate the two constructed dyads, or if the constructed dyads fail to have identical joint distributions.
  • The empirical relevance claim is refuted if, across existing interactive paradigms where the partner's cue is dissociable from the shared object (gaze-following with object occlusion, live versus recorded partner designs, dual-brain stimulation), report agreement and inter-brain synchrony turn out to track cross-agent interventional effects so tightly that the two never dissociate in practice, rendering the index idle.
  • The architectural claim is refuted if a shared-blackboard multi-agent system, with the blackboard clamped and no additional inter-agent monitoring routes, nonetheless exhibits cross-agent route-isolated rank greater than 1 under intervention on one agent's attentional target.
  • The clinical mapping is refuted if populations with well-documented joint-attention impairment show reduced cross-agent mediation only when object attention and report agreement are also reduced, indicating the index adds no dissociation beyond individual attention measures.

Planned Manuscript Structure

  • Abstract: Introduce shared attention as the paradigm of intersubjective consciousness, state the joint-versus-aligned question, describe the dyadic causal model and the underdetermination proposition, and give the bounded implication for human–AI and AI–AI dyads.
  • Research Question and Hypothesis: State the research question about interventional conditions for joint access and the Alignment Underdetermination Proposition with all quantities defined, and separate phenomenal, access, report, perceived, agency, and moral-status readings of shared attention.
  • Specialist Framework and Contribution: Explain how second-person neuroscience and intersubjectivity research change the unit of analysis from system to interaction, and present the Joint Access Index with its nested hierarchy of monitoring, common, mutual, and shared attention.
  • Evidence and Literature Synthesis: Synthesize developmental joint-attention studies, hyperscanning results and their common-stimulus critiques, dual-brain stimulation, autism and schizophrenia intersubjectivity findings, and the retrieved AI-consciousness debate, labeling each as evidence, inference, or speculation.
  • Cross-Disciplinary Integration: Integrate the relational versus reductive philosophical dispute, phenomenological accounts of we-experience, clinical phenomenology, and multi-agent AI architecture with the causal criterion, marking where each contributes a target, a bridge claim, or a boundary.
  • Analysis, Argument, or Model: Define the dyadic structural causal model, construct the common-cause and reciprocal dyads, prove passive indistinguishability and rank divergence, establish invariance and monotonicity of the index, and derive which components of J are forced to 1 in shared-workspace AI systems.
  • Alternatives, Counterarguments, and Boundary Cases: Address the reductive common-knowledge account, the primitive we-experience account, the partner-as-stimulus boundary ambiguity, asymmetric dyads, and the objection that the index measures joint agency rather than joint access.
  • Empirical Implications and Falsification Criteria: Specify what existing interactive paradigms already bear on J, what would refute the proposition, the relevance claim, the architectural claim, and the clinical mapping, and how perceived jointness should be measured separately from J.
  • Limitations: Acknowledge boundary-relativity of J, dependence on the feasibility of clamping the common input, the finite-state idealization, the absence of any bridge from J to phenomenal we-experience, the AI-generated and non-peer-reviewed status of the lab frameworks developed here, and the lack of a new experiment.
  • Conclusion: Conclude that shared attention is joint access only under a cross-agent interventional signature that aligned reports and synchrony cannot supply, that this reframes AI shared-consciousness questions at the dyad level, and that no value of the index settles phenomenality or moral status.
