A Failed Polarity-Separation Pilot in a Locally Labeled Qwen3.8-27B-4bit MLX Artifact: Frozen-Evidence Residual-Stream Steering of Prompt-Visibility Reports: Plain-English Explainer

A reader-friendly explanation of a failed attempt to separate an AI model's access signal from its YES/NO answering habit.

Plain-English explainer Machine Consciousness and Mechanistic Interpretability 09/02/2026, 07:46 PM

What problem is this paper trying to solve?

When a language model answers questions about what is visible in a prompt, it may look as if it knows what information it has access to. But the model might simply be following surface cues or developing a preference for a particular answer token.

Short answer

This paper tests whether a direction inside a local Qwen3.8 model tracks prompt visibility in a way that survives reversed YES/NO instructions. The result is mostly negative: the fitted direction showed a small access-coded effect, but its raw push toward YES was much larger. So the study did not demonstrate a clean internal access variable.

The Problem Being Tested

Imagine a prompt where one value is visible and another value is masked. The model is asked whether the queried value is available. If visible always means YES and masked always means NO, then an internal feature trained on the labels might just learn a YES-versus-NO policy.

To make that harder, the experiment used both direct and reversed mappings. In the reversed version, visible meant NO and masked meant YES. A genuine prompt-visibility signal should care about visibility rather than simply pushing one answer token.

What The Experiment Did

The study looked for a residual-stream direction inside a locally labeled Qwen3.8-27B-4bit MLX artifact. It trained on several prompt families, selected a layer using a validation family, and tested on a held-out packet family.

Then it added the fitted direction back into the model and measured how the YES and NO logits changed. The crucial comparison was whether the direction moved the model toward the answer that meant "accessible" under the current mapping, more than it simply moved the model toward YES.

What Was Found

The fitted direction achieved 0.625 accuracy on 8 expanded held-out rows, but the uncertainty was wide and the validation scan was weak. Its access-coded intervention slope was positive and larger than one orthogonal random control, which is the most favorable part of the result.

However, the raw YES-minus-NO slope was about 9.5 times larger than the access-coded slope. The correct-minus-incorrect slope was also negative. In plain terms, the strongest observed effect looked more like a YES-token push than a reliable access-sensitive intervention.

Why It Matters

This is useful precisely because it failed in an informative way. AI-consciousness tests often rely on reports, labels, or self-monitoring-like behavior. This paper shows that even careful counterbalancing may not remove answer-policy confounds.

The result does not say that language models lack access, self-monitoring, or consciousness. It says this particular frozen assay did not identify those things.

Key takeaways

  • Counterbalancing YES and NO is helpful, but not enough by itself.
  • A steerable direction can mix prompt cues, instructions, formatting, and output-token preference.
  • The safest reading is a failed methods pilot, not a discovery about machine consciousness.
